Briggs tested games alongside their writers-programmers. Testing included finding plausible player actions that a fictional world had not accounted for, not just crashes or coding errors. Romance was her own choice, not management's assignment. She wanted an adventure written from a female perspective and disliked the trivial cooking or sewing games then sold to girls. Some reviewers disliked the heroine's permitted actions and wanted a stronger fighter. Briggs regarded romance as a legitimate form, even when critics treated a woman writing for women as a limitation. Steve Meretzky encouraged her to apply as an implementer. Her application game used an abstract setting so realistic details would not expose gaps in her implementation skills. Infocom initially allowed writers considerable freedom. Briggs later remembered creative neglect and trust fondly; after Cornerstone failed and Activision acquired the company, layoffs and micromanagement changed her experience. She spent months cutting a completed Plundered Hearts to fit machines including the Commodore 64. She linked frequent player deaths to simplifying puzzles and remembered bugs that escaped testing. Management wanted another commercial hit, while her vampire and Doctor Who proposals went nowhere. Plundered Hearts sold about half Infocom's average; she eventually left amid demands without clear creative direction. Years later, players told her the game had mattered to them, despite its disappointing sales. She understood text adventures as closer to books than films, involving imagined environments and play.
