Testing fictional worlds: She tested plausible player actions that writers had not implemented, alongside ordinary software bugs. Describing a familiar desk makes players expect its ordinary objects; fantasy settings can avoid such expectations.
Choosing romance: Writing romance from a heroine's perspective was her choice, not a commission from management.
Players and the heroine: Some critics wanted a heroine who could fight, and attacked the restrictions on her actions.
Becoming an author: Her abstract application game avoided realistic details that might expose gaps in implementation.
Working conditions: Creative freedom and trust gave way to layoffs and micromanagement after Cornerstone's failure.
Editing and bugs: Fitting the completed game into limited computer memory required months of cuts; some bugs remained. She recalled a missed early bug involving the coffer sliding from under the bed and accepted responsibility.
Sales and next projects: Management demanded a hit but rejected her next proposals, including vampires and Doctor Who.
Readers and the medium: Low sales contrasted with players' lasting attachment; she compared text adventures to books.
